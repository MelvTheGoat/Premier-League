How the predictions are made

Two models, two jobs

The outcome model answers "who wins?" as a three-way probability. It is a gradient-boosted classifier blended with a multinomial logistic regression: the booster finds the interactions between contextual features, the linear model keeps the probabilities honest early in a season when the booster has little to go on.

The scoreline model answers a different question and uses a tool suited to it: a Dixon-Coles bivariate Poisson goal model, which gives each club an attack and a defence strength, applies a shared home advantage and a correction for low-scoring draws, and weights recent matches more heavily. Exact scores are much harder to call than outcomes, so the site shows the predicted and actual scores side by side without marking them right or wrong.

Context as features, not overrides

Nothing here nudges a prediction by hand. Every contextual signal is turned into a number and handed to the model, which decides for itself how much it is worth:

  • Form - points, goals and shots over the last 4, 6 and 10 games, across all competitions.
  • Strength - a cross-division Elo rating, so promoted clubs arrive with a real rating rather than a blank one.
  • Table context - position, and the points gap to the title, to Europe and to the relegation zone. What a club is playing for changes as the season goes on.
  • Fixture congestion - days of rest, matches in the last fortnight, midweek kick-offs and European commitments: the ingredients of rotation risk.
  • Manager - tenure length, games under the current manager, and the change in results either side of an appointment.
  • Availability - how many first-team regulars and key players are missing.
  • Squad quality - FIFA/EA FC squad ratings as a proxy for quality on paper, standardised within a season, with the age of the rating supplied so the model can discount a stale one.
  • Head to head - recent meetings, home and away splits, and how long since the two last met.

How the rolling retrain works

Predictions for gameweek N are made by a model trained only on matches played before that gameweek's first kick-off - no exceptions, in training or in serving. Once the gameweek finishes, its results are ingested and the model is retrained before gameweek N+1 is predicted, so every week the model has learned from the one before.

Predictions are never rewritten. Each run is stored with what it was trained on, so the history above shows what was actually forecast at the time rather than what today's model would say with hindsight.
